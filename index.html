My Projects

MyBoard

A whiteboard app I built because nothing else fit the way I work.

I collect reference images constantly — for drawings, game design, UI inspiration, worldbuilding — and I kept running into the same wall: every board app I tried had a free tier that got in my way eventually. So I built my own. MyBoard started as a simple image board and grew into a full whiteboarding application: an infinite canvas you can pan and zoom across, boards that nest inside other boards so large amounts of information stay organized instead of turning into a pile, rich text notes with their own floating style toolbar, and a color system with saved and recently-used swatches so I'm not re-picking the same colors over and over. I designed and built every part of it myself in C# and WPF, using it daily as both a real tool and a personal sandbox for learning software architecture and interface design at the same time. It's still actively evolving — markdown support, keyboard shortcuts, and export/import are next on the list.

Anito

A roguelike where Philippine mythology gets to be the main character.

Anito is a top-down pixel-art roguelike my team built as a research project, built around Filipino folklore — the kind of stories that don't get nearly enough space in games. My focus on the team was the visual side: designing the characters, enemies, and tilesets in Aseprite that gave the game its identity, making sure the art actually felt like it belonged to the mythology we were drawing from rather than generic fantasy reskinned. I also picked up some gameplay scripting in Unity along the way, though the art is where most of my time went. The project wasn't just "make a game" — it doubled as a research study, with survey and playtest data collected from 50 participants to see how players actually responded to the difficulty, the visuals, and the cultural framing. Watching real feedback shape a project I helped design for was one of the more useful parts of the whole process

UrbanGrow

Teaching a model to tell a sunny windowsill from a shady one.

UrbanGrow started from a simple, annoying problem: knowing which plants will actually survive in a given spot. Instead of guessing, I built a system that looks at a photo of your planting environment, classifies its light conditions, and recommends plants suited to it. Under the hood, it's a MobileNetV2 model I trained on nearly 2,750 labeled images across five light categories, landing at 85% test accuracy. The messier and more interesting part was everything around the model — cleaning the dataset, splitting it properly so results were trustworthy, and building a rule-based layer that turns a raw prediction into an actual plant recommendation someone could use. I wrote it all up as a full research paper, including where the model struggled most (turns out "partial sun" and "partial shade" look a lot alike to a camera).
